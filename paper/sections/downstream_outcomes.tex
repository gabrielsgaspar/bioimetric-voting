\section{Outcomes}
\label{sec:downstream-outcomes}

The administrative results in Sections~\ref{sec:electorate-size} and~\ref{sec:decomposition} pin down two changes that matter for any positive theory of local policy. First, after BVR the registered electorate is meaningfully smaller and tilted toward voters recorded as more educated: the low-education count falls sharply on impact, the high-education count rises, and re-labeling is identified in the aggregate. Second, the recadastramento process that produced that contraction also produced cleaner records. Voters who appeared in person to update their fingerprints simultaneously updated stale schooling fields, address fields, and the elementary fact of whether they could still be reached at all. From the perspective of local politicians and municipal bureaucrats, the post-BVR electorate is therefore not only smaller and more selected, but also more legible. The downstream question that organizes this section is which margins of local policy respond when the electorate that matters changes in exactly that way.

A long tradition in political economy argues that politicians weight the preferences of citizens who are visible, mobilized, and easy to reach \citep{Lindbeck1987BalancedBudgetRedistri,Dixit1996TheDeterminSuccess,besleyCoate1997}, and turnout-based representation arguments make the same point in a different language \citep{Hajnal2005WhereTurnoutMatters}. BVR provides a sharp shock to that visibility margin without redrawing the underlying population: who lives in the municipality has not changed; who the municipality can see, count, and contact has. The outcomes that fit this mechanism most directly are those where the marginal post-BVR voter is distinct from the marginal pre-BVR voter, and where the local state has direct levers. Two families of outcomes meet that test. The first is regular child-oriented schooling, which is salient to voters who pass through recadastramento and whose upward-corrected education records mark them as disproportionately attached to the formal school system. The second is visible local public goods that are easy to attribute to incumbents and easier to target once the resident population is better mapped \citep{ferrazFinan2011}. The outcomes that do not fit this mechanism --- broad national vote shares and system-wide health inputs --- are not predicted to respond first, and the evidence below disciplines the story by showing that they do not.

\subsection{A Tighter Registry Confirms the Contraction}
\label{ssec:downstream-political}

The most immediate downstream pattern is a direct confirmation that the registry contraction documented in Section~\ref{sec:electorate-size} is doing what the mechanism says it is doing. Turnout, measured as valid votes over registered voters, rises sharply at adoption --- by roughly $7.9$ percentage points in the unweighted estimates, with similar magnitudes at later horizons. BVR removes voters from the denominator without removing them from any behavioral margin that is conditional on remaining registered, so the post-treatment turnout series is shaped accordingly: an immediate jump on impact and persistent elevation, rather than a gradual diffusion of new participation. The pattern matches \citet{forquesatoRodrigues2023}, who find that Brazilian biometric recadastramento reduced registered voters without significantly changing the absolute number of votes cast. I do not interpret the turnout shift as a behavioral change. It is the mechanical signature of the same contraction documented above, and it pins down the population we should focus on when interpreting the policy outcomes that follow: the active, post-recadastramento electorate, not the pre-BVR rolls.

National presidential vote shares behave as this mechanism predicts they should --- that is, they do not move in a clean municipality-level way. A change in who is locally registered alters the composition of the active electorate that local politicians face, but it does not alter the determinants of national presidential cycles, which are dominated by economic conditions, candidate effects, and national campaigns common to treated and untreated municipalities alike \citep{Ferrer2023HowPartisanLocal,Marx2021VoterMobilisaAnd}. The absence of a national partisan realignment is therefore not a puzzle to explain away. It is the prediction the mechanism makes: if BVR shows up in politics anywhere, it should show up where local incumbents control the policy lever and where the post-BVR electorate is most visible. The rest of the section pursues exactly that prediction.

\subsection{Education and Child-Oriented Schooling}
\label{ssec:downstream-education}

The clearest downstream response is in child-oriented schooling, and it follows directly from who survives in the post-BVR registry. Section~\ref{sec:decomposition} establishes that part of the registry shift is the upward updating of stale education records: voters who completed recadastramento were disproportionately those whose schooling had progressed since their original registration. Those voters are also disproportionately working-age parents whose children pass through the municipal school system. The decomposition therefore implies that the politically active electorate after BVR is over-weighted toward households with concrete, observable stakes in regular primary and lower-secondary education. The political-economy literature on the determinants of public schooling makes the same prediction \citep{Meltzer1981RationalTheoryThe,Hunter2010}: when the active electorate tilts toward voters with children in the regular system, local schooling outcomes should move first.

Figure~\hyperref[fig:downstream-education]{\ref*{fig:downstream-education}} shows that they do. Per-capita spending on child and basic education rises by about $0.09$ log points on average over the post-BVR window, with a flat pre-period and a positive trajectory that strengthens at longer horizons. Municipal final-years elementary approval rises by about $1.9$ percentage points, and final-years abandonment falls by about $1.0$ percentage point. The point is not that municipalities reorganized their education systems from scratch. It is that the parts of the schooling system most legible to the post-BVR electorate received both more resources and better measurable outcomes, and they did so on the same margins where the registry composition shifted.

The contrast with \citet{Fujiwara2015VotingTechnoloPolitica} sharpens the mechanism. Electronic voting reduced the cost of casting a valid ballot at the polling place; it therefore raised the effective political weight of less-educated voters, and the local policy response --- visible in health spending and infant outcomes --- tilted toward groups that had been previously under-represented in the active electorate. BVR pushes the same lever in the opposite direction. It raises the cost of remaining on the register and tightens the link between the voter file and observable administrative attachment. The active electorate that emerges is more selected toward voters who are easier for local politicians to see, and the schooling response is exactly what their salient preferences predict. The supporting Brazilian evidence on electoral accountability is consistent with that reading: when incumbents observe an informative signal about their electorate, they respond \citep{ferrazFinan2011}.

\subsection{Visible Local Public Goods}
\label{ssec:downstream-public-goods}

The second pattern is in the kind of spending that local incumbents can most directly claim credit for. Figure~\hyperref[fig:downstream-public-goods]{\ref*{fig:downstream-public-goods}} reports two SICONFI per-capita spending outcomes --- annual infrastructure and two-year-cycle urbanism --- that rise after adoption, with post-period averages of roughly $0.05$ and $0.04$ log points and flat pre-periods. These are not transfer programs or diffuse social benefits. They are visible, locally attributable public goods whose presence is observable in the streets the post-BVR electorate walks down, the lighting it sees, and the paving, drainage, and small public works it uses on a daily basis.

Two complementary mechanisms fit this pattern, and the registry results in Sections~\ref{sec:electorate-size} and~\ref{sec:decomposition} give both bite. The first is electoral: a smaller, more educated, and more administratively attached electorate values visible local goods more than diffuse transfers, and incumbents who want credit at the next election respond on exactly the margin where credit is easiest to claim \citep{Lindbeck1987BalancedBudgetRedistri,Dixit1996TheDeterminSuccess}. The second is operational. Recadastramento required municipalities to set up local service points, contact citizens individually, and coordinate with the electoral bureaucracy, and that process plausibly improved the local state's overview of where citizens actually reside and which neighborhoods it can reach. A sharper map of the resident population makes complementary place-based investments easier to target and easier to justify. Comparable biometric evidence from India shows that authentication and information technologies can improve service delivery and reduce leakage precisely when they raise the quality of administrative records \citep{Muralidharan2016BuildingStateCapacity,Bossuroy2024BiometriMonitoriService}, and a broader state-capacity literature emphasizes that legibility and effective governance reinforce each other \citep{besleyPersson2011,AcemogluRobinson2019}.

The level-spending result in Figure~\hyperref[fig:downstream-public-goods]{\ref*{fig:downstream-public-goods}} is consistent with both channels, and the two are reinforcing rather than competing. The cleanest reading is a scale-and-capacity story: post-BVR municipalities spend more in the visible categories the new electorate is most attuned to, without obviously starving other functions. That is exactly the joint prediction of a more demanding active electorate and a local state with a clearer view of the population it is delivering to.

\subsection{Birth and Health Outcomes}
\label{ssec:downstream-health}

The health evidence is narrower than the education and infrastructure evidence, and that narrowness is itself consistent with the mechanism rather than a problem for it. Figure~\hyperref[fig:downstream-health]{\ref*{fig:downstream-health}} reports three outcomes whose direction and magnitude line up with the post-BVR legibility channel rather than with a generalized improvement in the health system. BCG coverage rises modestly. The share of low-education mothers with adequate prenatal visits also rises. The share of births to teen mothers increases by about one percentage point on average over the post window, with a flat pre-period and a steady positive trajectory at longer horizons.

These three results sit naturally alongside the schooling and infrastructure findings once the legibility channel is taken seriously. BCG and prenatal-visit measures pick up routine, scheduled contact between vulnerable families and the local health bureaucracy; the families most likely to benefit from a denser administrative footprint are exactly those whose contact with the local state had been most marginal before recadastramento. The teen-mother share is more striking and could be misread, so it deserves an explicit interpretation. I treat it as most plausibly a measurement-visibility effect of the same recadastramento-driven thickening of local administrative contact that drives the rest of the section. SINASC birth records are more complete in places where the local state interacts more intensively with low-resource families, and a reading in which BVR causally raised teen pregnancy is hard to reconcile with the simultaneous improvement in low-education prenatal care and routine immunization, both of which point the other way. The compositional reading --- that treated municipalities changed in whose births are recorded rather than in who gives birth --- is the same legibility story applied to a vital-statistics outcome.

What is absent from the health block is equally important for the interpretation. BVR did not deliver a broad expansion of primary care, a system-wide change in hospital inputs, or the kind of large general-purpose health improvement documented in the electronic-voting setting of \citet{Fujiwara2015VotingTechnoloPolitica}. That absence is consistent with the section's central claim. BVR did not expand the local state in scale; it changed which residents the local state can most easily observe. The health outcomes that move are precisely those most sensitive to that observation margin, and the absence of broader system-wide movement is exactly what the mechanism predicts.

\subsection{A More Legible Electorate}
\label{ssec:downstream-mechanism}

The pieces of the downstream evidence converge on a single mechanism. The administrative results in Sections~\ref{sec:electorate-size} and~\ref{sec:decomposition} show that BVR contracts the registered electorate and updates the records of those who remain, and both channels point in the same direction for the local political principal: after BVR, the active electorate is smaller, more educated on paper, more administratively attached, and easier to read against the resident population. In a probabilistic-voting framework, the equilibrium policy weights groups that are visible, mobilized, and reachable \citep{Lindbeck1987BalancedBudgetRedistri,Dixit1996TheDeterminSuccess,Hajnal2005WhereTurnoutMatters}, and the existing Brazilian evidence on electoral accountability suggests that incumbents do respond to informative signals about their constituents \citep{ferrazFinan2011}. The outcomes that move in this section are exactly those the mechanism predicts will move first. Regular child schooling is salient to a post-recadastramento electorate disproportionately composed of attached parents. Infrastructure and urbanism are visible, locally attributable, and easier to target once the resident population is better mapped. Routine maternal and immunization outcomes among vulnerable families are sensitive to administrative contact density. The outcomes that do not move are also the ones the mechanism would not predict to respond first: national-cycle-dominated presidential vote shares and broad health-system inputs. Related evidence from restrictions on fraudulent registration in other settings shows that comparable shocks to the active electorate can shift candidate selection and public-goods provision \citep{Karim2025VoterBuyingPolitici}, and the patterns documented here are consistent with the same broader logic.

The most parsimonious reading is that BVR worked on both sides of the principal-agent problem at once. It changed which citizens local politicians have an incentive to court, and it changed which citizens the municipal bureaucracy can identify, locate, and reach. The model in Section~\ref{sec:theory} treats this as a single shift in the legibility of the electorate, and the data are consistent with that reduced form. The section does not argue that BVR rewrote Brazilian local government. The effects, where they appear, are modest. The argument is the cleaner and more disciplined one: BVR changed the part of the electorate that the local state can most easily observe, and local policy responded along precisely the margins where that observability matters most.

\subsection{Interpretation and Limits}
\label{ssec:downstream-limits}

Three caveats discipline this reading. The first concerns the fiscal channel. The level-spending response in infrastructure and urbanism is cleaner than any response in the corresponding budget shares, which means the evidence supports a scale-and-capacity reading --- more resources flowing to visible functions --- rather than an active reallocation of the municipal budget away from other categories. That is consistent with the mechanism but should not be overstated: a stronger version of the claim would require a clean reallocation result, and the data do not deliver one. The second caveat is that the LAPOP trust evidence in Section~\ref{sec:political-trust} can be read through the same legibility lens. The post-BVR electorate is more selectively composed, and any observed shift in trust among lower-education respondents partly reflects that selection rather than a within-person belief change. The two sections should therefore be read together, not as independent tests. The third caveat is interpretive. The downstream evidence shows that local policy moves along the margins the mechanism predicts; it does not separately identify, within those margins, what share of the response is electoral incentive and what share is administrative capacity. These channels are reinforcing rather than competing, and the joint reduced form is what this section identifies and what the theoretical model in Section~\ref{sec:theory} uses.
