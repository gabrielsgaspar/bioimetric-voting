# BJS Ref-0 Downstream Findings and Mechanism Interpretation

Date: 2026-05-19

This note summarizes the downstream Borusyak-Jaravel-Spiess (BJS) / imputation difference-in-differences screen generated by `src/analysis/run_downstream_bjs_ref0_nonhybrid_screen.py`. The screen estimates downstream municipal outcomes after strict biometric voter registration (BVR), excluding municipalities ever flagged as hybrid (`ever_hybrid_bvr == 1`) and clustering standard errors at `municipality_id`. The event-study window is -8 to +8. All paths are normalized to event time 0 by subtracting the event-time-0 estimate from every event-time coefficient and setting event time 0 to zero in the plotted path.

The main output files are:

- Ranking table: `resources/tables/downstream_bjs_ref0_nonhybrid/master_bjs_ref0_nonhybrid_ranking.csv`
- Stacked event-study table: `resources/tables/downstream_bjs_ref0_nonhybrid/master_bjs_ref0_nonhybrid_event_study.csv`
- Additional diagnostics used for this note: `resources/tables/downstream_bjs_ref0_nonhybrid/bjs_ref0_report_augmented_diagnostics.csv`
- Combined plots: `resources/figures/downstream_bjs_ref0_nonhybrid/`
- Estimator outputs: `resources/did/downstream_bjs_ref0_nonhybrid/`

## How to Read the Evidence

The screen is exploratory. It should be used to identify candidate mechanisms and robustness targets, not to make final paper claims without additional checks. Three features matter for interpretation.

First, the pretrend label is a diagnostic screen. A result is labeled `pass` when there are at least three pre-period estimates, no pre-period estimate with `p < 0.05`, and a maximum absolute pre-period t-statistic no larger than 1.96. A result is labeled `near` when there are at least two pre-period estimates, no more than one pre-period estimate with `p < 0.05`, and a maximum absolute pre-period t-statistic no larger than 2.58. All other estimated outcomes are labeled `fail`.

Second, the main post-treatment magnitude reported here is `post_mean_event_1_8_ref0`: the average ref-0-normalized event-study estimate over event times 1 through 8. For annual outcomes this averages annual post-BVR event times. For biennial IDEB/SAEB outcomes and two-year SICONFI cycle outcomes, the event steps are two years. Because the ref-0 normalization subtracts the event-time-0 estimate after estimation, the post mean is a screening statistic, not a separately estimated aggregate treatment effect with its own covariance matrix.

Third, this note sometimes reports the number of post-period raw BJS event estimates with `p < 0.05`. Those p-values come from the raw BJS event estimates before ref-0 normalization. They are useful for judging whether a path has post-period signal, but they are not exact p-values for the ref-0-normalized contrasts. I therefore treat them as descriptive precision diagnostics.

## Education and Child-Oriented Policy

The clearest substantive pattern is in child-oriented education outcomes. Several education and spending outcomes have acceptable pretrends and move in a direction consistent with more attention to regular schooling after BVR. The adult/EJA panel shows clean positive movement for child/basic education spending per capita. The education panel shows higher SICONFI education spending per capita, higher final-years elementary approval, lower final-years abandonment, lower repetition or reprobation in selected outcomes, and positive final-years IDEB approval indicators.

This pattern is consistent with a representation channel. The main administrative results in the project show that strict BVR contracted the registered electorate and shifted its recorded education composition. If the reform changed who remained politically represented, then the local median active voter or politically salient voter may have changed as well. One plausible interpretation is that BVR raised the relative political weight of voters with stronger attachment to formal local institutions and to regular child schooling. In that case, municipal policy could tilt toward services used by households with children in the regular school system.

This interpretation is strengthened by the contrast with adult/EJA outcomes. The BJS screen does not provide clean evidence that EJA enrollment, EJA teachers, or EJA spending decline after BVR. Most adult/EJA count outcomes fail pretrends. What survives is the positive child/basic spending side. The cleaner claim is therefore not that BVR clearly cut adult education. It is that the screen finds more support for increased child/basic education resources than for an adult-education channel.

The education-flow estimates are also important because they point to outcomes rather than just spending. If approval rises and abandonment falls in selected school levels, the downstream pattern is not purely accounting. It may reflect either better-funded schools, improved municipal attention to school administration, or broader complementary investments that reduce barriers to continued schooling. These interpretations are still provisional because many education outcomes fail pretrends, but the pass/near subset is coherent enough to justify targeted follow-up.

## Complementary Infrastructure and Municipal Capacity

The SICONFI log per-capita spending results provide a second candidate mechanism. Annual infrastructure spending per capita rises by 0.048 log points with a pass pretrend. The two-year-cycle infrastructure result is larger at 0.077 log points and near-clean. Two-year-cycle urbanism spending rises by 0.041 log points with a pass pretrend. These are not enormous effects, but they are persistent and better behaved than most downstream spending outcomes.

This pattern fits an administrative-capacity or population-overview mechanism. BVR was not only a voting technology. It required recadastramento, biometric data collection, local service points, communication with voters, and coordination between electoral offices and municipalities. Even if municipal governments did not directly control the TSE/TRE rollout, the reform may have increased local knowledge about who was present, who could be reached, and which places had weak administrative contact with citizens. Better information about the resident population could make complementary municipal investments more attractive or easier to target.

A second interpretation is electoral. If BVR changed the active electorate, the newly salient electorate may have rewarded visible municipal public goods. Infrastructure and urbanism spending are relatively visible and broadly valued. They are also plausible complements to school access and local service delivery. This makes them natural candidates for a policy response to a post-BVR electorate that is more formally attached and easier for local officials to observe.

The spending-share results keep this story disciplined. No SICONFI spending-share outcome passes the pretrend screen. Several infrastructure, urbanism, and education shares are near-clean, but their magnitudes are small. This means the BJS evidence is stronger for higher spending levels in selected categories than for a clean reallocation of the municipal budget toward those categories. The mechanism should therefore be stated as a possible increase in complementary spending or fiscal capacity, not as a proven budget-share reallocation.

## Birth and Health Outcomes

The birth and health results are more mixed. The most striking signal is the teen mother share among all births, which rises by about 1.0 percentage point in the ref-0 post window. The post-period path is persistent and six post-period raw estimates have `p < 0.05`, but the pretrend label is `near` rather than `pass` because one pre-period diagnostic is marginal. This outcome deserves attention because it may connect BVR to education, social inclusion, and the age composition of political representation.

There are at least three possible interpretations of the teen-mother result. One is a social-policy interpretation: if BVR changed local political incentives away from marginal adult and youth services and toward the regular child-schooling population, some vulnerable young women may have received less effective support. A second is a measurement/contact interpretation: BVR and related administrative activity may have increased documentation, formal contact, or registration of vulnerable families, making teen motherhood more visible in official birth records. A third is a composition interpretation: treated municipalities may have changed in who gives birth, who remains in the municipality, or whose births are recorded. The BJS screen alone cannot distinguish these explanations.

The broader health evidence does not support a simple improvement story. Some IEPS outcomes pass pretrends, but they point in different directions. CSAP mortality falls on average, avoidable mortality rises, SUS beds fall, ICU beds rise, and BCG coverage increases modestly. Low-education adequate prenatal visits has a clean pretrend but weak post-period persistence. Primary-care and many immunization outcomes either fail pretrends or do not form a coherent pattern. The most defensible statement is that birth/health outcomes contain selected signals, not a broad health-system response.

## What the Results Suggest About Mechanisms

The best-fitting story is a two-part mechanism. First, BVR changes political representation by contracting and re-sorting the active electorate. This changes which residents are electorally salient to local officials. Second, the reform improves administrative contact and information about the local population, either directly through recadastramento or indirectly through the local political response to the rollout. The downstream results that best fit this combined story are child/basic education spending, selected school-flow improvements, and infrastructure/urbanism spending.

This mechanism is stronger than a pure access-cost story. If the main downstream channel were simply that municipalities improved school access, the Censo Escolar transport outcomes should show cleaner post-BVR improvements. They do not. Almost all school transport and residence-access outcomes fail pretrends, and the only near-clean school-access outcome is tiny. Similarly, if BVR triggered a broad health-administration upgrade, primary-care coverage, prenatal care, mortality, and immunization outcomes should move more coherently. They do not.

The most promising interpretation is therefore not "BVR improved all public services." It is more specific: BVR may have changed the political and informational environment in ways that favored visible local public goods and child-oriented schooling, while leaving many direct access and health outcomes unaffected or too confounded by pre-existing trends to interpret.

## Candidate Paper Language

The BJS downstream screen provides exploratory evidence that the consequences of BVR may extend beyond the electoral register. In the nonhybrid strict-BVR sample, the most stable downstream patterns appear in child-oriented education spending, selected school-flow outcomes, and complementary municipal infrastructure or urbanism spending. These outcomes have comparatively clean pretrends and move after BVR in directions consistent with greater municipal attention to regular schooling and visible local public goods. At the same time, most downstream outcomes fail pretrend screens, and the strongest results should be interpreted as candidates for mechanism analysis rather than final causal estimates.

This pattern is consistent with a representation-and-information mechanism. BVR tightened the registry and changed the composition of the administratively represented electorate. The same process may also have improved local administrative contact with citizens. If local officials responded to a different electorate and a clearer picture of the resident population, then spending and administrative attention could shift toward regular child schooling and complementary infrastructure. The null and failed-pretrend results are equally informative: they suggest that the downstream response was not a broad improvement in all public services, not a clean school-transport access shock, and not a general health-system expansion.

## Recommended Follow-Up

1. Re-estimate the most promising pass/near outcomes with alternative estimators already used in the paper, especially Callaway-Sant'Anna and Sun-Abraham, using the same strict nonhybrid sample.
2. For education outcomes, test sensitivity to dropping 2020 and to network definitions (municipal, public, public estadual+municipal).
3. For infrastructure and urbanism, compare log per-capita effects with revenue denominators, total spending denominators, and two-year-cycle aggregation.
4. For teen motherhood, test whether the result is concentrated by mother education, age bins, municipality size, baseline registry contraction, or baseline education composition.
5. For the representation mechanism, interact the promising downstream outcomes with the size of the registry contraction and the low-education electorate shift.
6. For the information mechanism, interact the promising downstream outcomes with local access, communication, mutirao, or TRE/TSE service-intensity measures once those are validated.
